Notes

What’s real here, and what isn’t.

What this is

A computer the size of a moon, imagined on Triton. It starts in 2050 as one hall beside a dish near the south pole and grows for under forty years, until by about 2087 trunk lines run over the whole moon and radiator fields stand along them. You arrive on the ground at the first landing field; go back to orbit and drag the year to watch it grow.

Power: fusion (fuel from Triton’s ice and Neptune’s air). The white fins are radiators, dumping waste heat into a 38 K sky. The domes beside each avenue are the fusion plants.

The gold wire is the mind. In daylight every shape gets its wire, like the enamel it’s drawn as. At night only the network keeps its gold, and the pulses on it are results heading home to the dish.

What’s real

Triton is 2,707 km across. It keeps one face to Neptune and goes round it backwards. The ground is about 38 K (−235 °C) under a thin nitrogen air, about 14 millionths of Earth’s. Gravity is 0.78 m/s², about a twelfth of ours, so the horizon is only 2.3 km away when you stand up.

The pink-white south polar cap, the dark streaks across it, the dimpled “cantaloupe” ground (cells 25–35 km across), the smoother plains and the blue band at the cap’s edge are all from Voyager 2’s pictures in 1989. So are the geysers: columns of nitrogen and dust that rise about 8 km, then trail more than 100 km downwind. Voyager saw only about 40% of Triton well. The rest here follows the same patterns but is a guess.

The sky is worked out for today’s date. From the landing site Neptune hangs 30° up, due north, 8° across, and never moves. The Sun goes round once every 5 days 21 hours; at this season it never sets near the south pole. Earth is about four light-hours away. You arrive at the next moment the Sun stands about 27° up over the landing site (within six days), so the ice and the fins are lit and Neptune is about half lit; the Sky line under the place name says when that is, and switches to today’s sky.

What’s imagined

The whole machine: the dish, the halls, the radiators, the network and the numbers. Nobody is planning it.

How the numbers are worked out

Power is set by the radiators. Sunlight out here is 1/900 of Earth’s, so the power is fusion: deuterium from Triton’s water ice, and deuterium and helium-3 from Neptune’s air. Every watt of it ends up as heat, and in a vacuum the only way to lose heat is to glow. So the fins decide how much power the moon can use.

Each fin face sheds about 270 W/m². The fins run warm, at 330 K (57 °C). A surface that warm, with emissivity 0.9, glows away εσT⁴ ≈ 605 W per square metre. But the fins stand 390 m tall in rows 250 m apart, so the next row fills about 55% of each face’s view (for parallel plates that share is √(1+(250/390)²) − 250/390), and heat sent there comes straight back. About 45% escapes: ≈ 272 W/m², on both faces of every panel.

Fusion power = fin area × 272 W/m². Each square metre of field holds about 2.7 m² of panel face. At full size, about 2087, that is 2.5 million km² of panel face shedding about 0.68 PW: 36 times all the power humanity uses today (about 19 TW).

Compute = electricity ÷ energy per operation. The plants turn about half their heat into electricity. The logic is superconducting and reversible, and this is where the cold helps. Erasing a bit costs at least kT·ln 2, which is 3.6×10⁻²² J at Triton’s 38 K, eight times less than at room temperature. Superconducting wire, which carries signals without loss, works here with no refrigerator, because some superconductors keep working up to 92 K. I assume the 2080s logic spends 10⁻²⁰ J per operation all in, including keeping it cold: about 30 times that floor. Today’s best AI chips spend about 10⁻¹² J. So at full size: 0.34 PW ÷ 10⁻²⁰ J ≈ 3×10³⁴ operations a second, about 10¹⁴ times today’s largest AI clusters (around 10²⁰ a second).

At full size the machine sheds about 330 times as much heat as Triton gets from the Sun. A real one would warm its own ice and air, and would have to deal with that.

Some things are drawn bigger than life so they read. Neptune’s rings are far more visible than they really are. From orbit, the enamel cells and the trunk lines are enlarged. The glow and the pulses show where computation flows; real cables don’t glow. The pulses are slowed down enormously: a real signal would cross Triton in about 21 thousandths of a second.

Why Triton?

A computer only needs to be close if someone is waiting for it. Long jobs can wait four hours for a question and four more for the answer. Out here there’s room, cold and quiet.

Controls

In orbit: drag to turn, scroll or pinch to zoom, click Triton to land. On the ground: click to look with the mouse (or drag), W A S D to move, Shift to bound, Space to jump, F to fly, Space and Q to climb and sink, scroll for flying speed, O for orbit. Anywhere: G grows the mind, M opens the atlas, P saves a picture, H hides the interface. On a phone, use the left pad to move and drag to look.
