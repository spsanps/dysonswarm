Terraforming Venus

Shade it from the Sun, freeze its air, pave over the ice, and build a world on top.

Paul Birch worked out in 1991 how this could be done in about two hundred years. You do it here, one step at a time, standing on a real headland above a real bay, or from orbit.

Notes

How this is meant to work

This follows Paul Birch’s 1991 paper “Terraforming Venus Quickly” (Journal of the British Interplanetary Society 44, 157–167). Year 0 is his 2040, and most of the dates are his.

  1. The shade. A mirror about twice as wide as Venus, a quarter of the way to the Sun, sends the sunlight past the planet. Venus cools by radiating into space.
  2. Rain. The sulphuric acid clouds rain out. Below 31 °C, carbon dioxide condenses and rains as a liquid into the lowlands.
  3. CO2 snow. At −57 °C and 5 bar (the triple point) the seas freeze. The rest falls as snow, leaving about two bar of nitrogen.
  4. Paving. Birch covers the frozen seas with hollow blocks or foamed rock, lowered from floating colonies, so they stay frozen when the light returns. Covering the land too, with plastic sheet, is my addition.
  5. Soil. Laying soil on top of the paving is also mine. A soletta, a mirror in a 24-hour orbit, gives Venus a day; Venus itself turns once in 243 days.
  6. Water. An ice moon (Birch suggests Enceladus) is broken up and dropped in over decades.
  7. Green. Plants turn the leftover carbon dioxide into oxygen. People live under tents until the air is breathable, and the floating colonies come down onto the sea.

What you do

Each stage has one button. Some just let the years pass. Two are work: lay the paving and spread the soil by hand, one hexagon at a time (each is 1.2 km across), before the colonies’ machines take over. Fly over the plains with F, or go up to orbit to see the shade between Venus and the Sun and the whole planet change.

What is real here

The land is real at large scale: NASA’s Magellan radar altimetry of Ishtar Terra (4.6 km per pixel, public domain), around 61.6° N, 5° W, south-west of Maxwell Montes. The bay below you is where Magellan’s lowlands are, and the CO2 sea level comes from Magellan’s global elevations. Heights are drawn 1.8 times taller than they are, as space painters always did. The headland, the rocks, the hexagons and everything smaller are invented. The globe in orbit uses the same Magellan data.

The air is a rough model: scattering grows with how much gas is above you, so the sky turns blue only once the carbon dioxide is gone. The CO2 condenses along its real vapour-pressure curve. Two things are painted, not measured. Today’s air is drawn about fifteen times clearer than it is; really you would see only a few kilometres into the orange. And the long night is lit as if by moonlight so you can see; Venus has no moon, and under the shade it would be close to black.

What is imagined

All of it as a plan. Birch’s paper is an engineering sketch, not a validated design, and nobody has checked how fast any of this could go. His paper also has heat pipes to cool the deep air, floating colonies at the one-bar level lit by their own small mirrors, and floaters that build the cover disks; they are all here, though their look is invented. Covering the land with plastic sheet and then soil is my addition to his plan. The town, the farms, the colony’s shape and the timing of the land stages are invented too.
